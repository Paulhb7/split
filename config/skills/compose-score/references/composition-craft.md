# Composition craft — the rules behind a beautiful melody

This is the deep version of *The Rules* in `SKILL.md`. It's not theory for its
own sake — every rule is a lever you pull while writing the ABC. Beauty in tonal
music is not mysterious: it is **unity + variety + a single emotional arc**,
delivered through melody, harmony, and the tension between consonance and
dissonance. Master these and you stop writing "notes in a key" and start writing
music.

---

## 1. Melody — the line that sings

A melody is a **shape in time**, not a sequence of correct pitches. Test every
phrase you write against these.

### Contour and the single climax
- A phrase has **one high point**. Place the climax around the **golden section
  — roughly 2/3 of the way through** — and approach/leave it by step. A line
  with five equal peaks has no peak; the ear can't find the moment.
- The overall piece has a **structural climax** too — the highest, loudest, most
  harmonically charged instant. Save it. Don't blow your highest note in bar 2.

### Intervals — steps, leaps, and gap-fill
- **Conjunct (stepwise) motion is the default** — it's singable and smooth. Most
  of a good melody moves by 2nds.
- A **leap is an event**; it spends energy and creates expectation. The classic
  rule: **after a leap (especially a 4th or larger), reverse direction and fill
  the gap by step** ("gap-fill"). A leap up then a stepwise descent feels
  resolved; two big leaps in the same direction feel unhinged (unless you mean
  to).
- Leaps that outline a **chord** (arpeggiation) sound grounded; leaps to
  dissonant intervals (7ths, tritones) sound expressive/anxious — use on purpose.

### Motif → repetition → sequence → development
This is the engine of memorability. ~80% of what makes music "catchy".
1. **State a motif** — a tiny memorable cell, 2–4 notes or one bar. Give it a
   distinctive **rhythm**, not just pitches (think of the rhythm of the opening
   of Beethoven's 5th — four notes, one rhythm, a whole symphony).
2. **Repeat it** — literally, so the ear grabs it.
3. **Sequence it** — restate the same shape starting a step (or third) higher or
   lower. A rising sequence builds; a falling sequence relaxes. 2–3 steps of a
   sequence is ideal — a 4th step starts to feel mechanical.
4. **Develop it** — invert it, augment/diminish the rhythm, fragment it (keep
   the rhythm, change pitches), extend it. Variation keeps the idea alive without
   abandoning it.

> The discipline: **almost nothing should be brand-new after the first phrase.**
> Coherence comes from everything being a relative of the opening idea.

### Phrasing — question and answer (the period)
- Think in **phrases**, usually **4 or 8 bars**, with a breath (rest / long note)
  between them. Music without breath suffocates.
- The strongest small structure is the **period**:
  - **Antecedent** (the question): ends *open*, on a **half cadence (… → V)** —
    it sounds unfinished, leaning forward.
  - **Consequent** (the answer): often begins like the antecedent (so they
    rhyme), then ends *closed*, on an **authentic cadence (V → I)** — home.
- This open→closed pairing is why a melody feels like a complete *sentence*
  rather than a list.

### Melodic rhythm
- **Vary note durations.** All-equal values are dull; total randomness is noise.
  Establish a rhythmic motif and reuse it.
- **Agogic stress**: a longer note draws the ear — put length on the note you
  want to feel as the goal (often the climax or a resolution).
- **Anticipation / syncopation** off the beat gives life and forward lean. Place
  arrivals on strong beats, escapes off them.
- **Start and end well**: open a phrase on a **chord tone** (1, 3, or 5 — or a
  pickup leaning into one), end phrases on stable tones, end the **piece on the
  tonic** (scale degree 1), usually approached by step from above (2→1) or by the
  leading tone (7→1).

### Tension — the beauty is in the non-chord tones
Pure chord tones are safe and bland. The expressive moments are the notes that
**don't** belong to the chord and then resolve:
- **Passing tone** — steps between two chord tones (smooths a leap into a line).
- **Neighbor tone** — steps away and back (a little turn).
- **Suspension** — hold a note from the previous chord into the next, where it's
  now a dissonance, then **resolve it down by step** (the classic 4–3, 7–6, 9–8).
  This is *the* sound of longing in tonal music.
- **Appoggiatura** — leap *to* a non-chord tone on a strong beat, then step down
  to the chord tone. The "lean and sigh". Most achingly beautiful melodic moments
  are appoggiaturas (the held dissonant note that resolves).
- **Anticipation** — arrive at the next chord tone early.

> If a melody feels flat, it's usually **all chord tones**. Add a suspension or
> an appoggiatura at the phrase's emotional peak and it comes alive.

## 2. Harmony — the gravity underneath

### Functional progression
Diatonic chords group by **function**, and tonal music flows through them:
- **Tonic (T)**: I, vi — home, rest.
- **Predominant (PD)**: ii, IV — departure, builds toward the dominant.
- **Dominant (D)**: V, V7, vii° — maximum tension, *wants* to resolve to I.

The default current is **T → PD → D → T** (e.g. `I – IV – V7 – I`, or
`I – vi – ii – V – I`). The **V → I authentic cadence** is gravity itself: the
leading tone (scale degree 7) pulls up to 1, the 7th of V7 pulls down to 3.

### Cadences — punctuation
- **Authentic (V→I)**: full stop. Perfect (PAC) when both are in root position and
  the melody lands on 1 — use to end the piece.
- **Half (…→V)**: comma — ends open (the antecedent's cadence).
- **Deceptive (V→vi)**: the surprise — you expect home, you get the relative
  minor. Use it to **delay** the final resolution and create ache, then land the
  real V→I a phrase later.
- **Plagal (IV→I)**: the "amen", a soft afterglow after the authentic cadence.

### Harmonic rhythm
- The **rate chords change** is a tension lever. Slow harmonic rhythm = calm;
  speeding it up toward a cadence = drive. Keep one chord per bar as a baseline;
  push to two-per-bar approaching the climax.

### Color (use sparingly — one spice at a time)
- **Secondary dominant** (V/V, V/ii…): borrow the dominant of a chord that isn't
  the tonic to tonicize it — instant lift and direction.
- **Borrowed chords** (modal mixture): a **minor iv in a major key** is the
  bittersweet classic; ♭VI, ♭VII for warmth/drama.
- **Sevenths and ninths**: add warmth (maj7 = dreamy, dom7 = drive, min7 =
  mellow). A jazzily-voiced ii7–V7–Imaj7 is lush.
- The **Neapolitan (♭II6)** and **augmented sixth** chords are strong pre-dominant
  colors for Romantic intensity — powerful, rare.

### Modulation (for longer pieces)
- Move to a closely related key (the dominant, the relative major/minor) via a
  **pivot chord** shared by both keys, confirmed by a cadence in the new key.
- A middle section in a contrasting key (e.g. B section in the relative minor)
  is the simplest way to create large-scale variety; return home for the reprise.

## 3. Voice-leading — how the parts move

Independent lines that move smoothly = a rich, "professional" sound. Even in a
piano texture, think of the inner notes as **voices**.
- **Move each voice the smallest distance.** Keep common tones between chords;
  step the rest. Big simultaneous jumps in every voice sound clumsy.
- **Tendency tones resolve**: leading tone (7) → tonic (1) up; the 7th of a V7 →
  3 down; suspensions down by step.
- **Avoid parallel perfect 5ths and octaves** between any two voices — they
  fuse the voices and kill independence. Contrary or oblique motion is safest.
- **Bass is a melody.** Give it shape; use **inversions** (chord's 3rd or 5th in
  the bass) so it can walk by step (e.g. `I – V6 – vi` gives a stepwise bass
  1–7–6) instead of leaping between roots.
- **Spacing**: keep upper voices within an octave of each other; let the bass sit
  lower. Don't bunch everything in the middle (muddy) or strand a voice far away.

## 4. Form — the architecture

Small forms carry a short piece; pick one and let motivic unity do the rest.
- **Period** (a + a′): two phrases, question/answer (above). The basic 8-bar unit.
- **Binary (A B)**: two sections, often each repeated. B contrasts (new key/idea).
- **Rounded binary (A B A′)**: B contrasts, then A returns (shortened) — the seed
  of sonata form, very satisfying.
- **Ternary (A B A)**: a full contrasting middle, then a literal/varied return.
  The default for a song or a nocturne.
- **Theme and variations**: state a clear theme, then re-clothe it (new rhythm,
  register, mode, accompaniment) — great for showing range.
- **Rondo (A B A C A …)**: the **refrain form**. The **A section IS the refrain**
  (the "rondo theme") — it returns *intact* between contrasting episodes (B, C…).
  The ear learns it, then *welcomes its return* each time. The most satisfying way
  to build a memorable recurring idea in an instrumental piece. ABACA is the
  classic 5-part shape.
- **Song form (verse–chorus)**: the modern refrain. **Verse** (narrative, lower
  energy, the line that changes) → **Chorus** (the **hook** — the highest,
  catchiest, most harmonically stable phrase, returning *identical* each time,
  carrying the "title" gesture) → verse → chorus → **bridge** (contrast, departs
  to a new area) → final chorus, usually **lifted** (up an octave, fuller texture).
  The chorus is the payoff the verse sets up.

**The refrain principle** (rondo's A, the song's chorus): a section the listener
*anticipates*. Make the refrain the **most memorable, most stable, most singable**
material, and make the episodes/verses **depart** from it (new key, lower energy,
restless rhythm) so its return feels like *coming home*. Vary the **last** return
(higher, fuller, or a deceptive twist into the final cadence) so it's familiar but
never mechanical. Contrast is what makes a refrain land — a refrain with nothing
to come home *from* is just repetition.

**Unity across the form**: derive B from A where you can (same motif, new
treatment). Mark the return of A so the ear feels "home". The **structural climax
usually sits late** — near the end of B or at the return — not at the start.

## 5. The emotional arc (the thing people actually feel)

Zoom out from notes and chords: a beautiful piece is a **single journey**.
1. **Establish** — state the key, the mood, the motif. Stability.
2. **Depart & build** — sequences rise, harmonic rhythm quickens, the line
   climbs, dissonance increases, you move away from home (a new key or the
   dominant).
3. **Climax** — the highest/most charged moment, usually ~2/3–3/4 through, often
   on a suspension/appoggiatura over a dominant or a deceptive cadence.
4. **Resolve** — the line falls, the harmony finds V→I, you return home. A plagal
   "amen" or a quiet echo of the motif as an afterglow.

If you can't point to *where the tension peaks and where it resolves*, the piece
has no arc — and no amount of correct notes will make it beautiful.

## 6. A worked micro-example (D minor, 8-bar period)

The thinking, not just the notes:
- **Plan**: D minor, ♩=66, 3/4, tender. 8-bar period. LH = broken chords.
- **Motif (bar 1, RH)**: `A – D – F` rising (a 5th up then a 3rd) — outlines the
  tonic, ends on the calm 3rd. Distinctive rhythm: quarter, two eighths.
- **Bars 1–2**: state motif, then a falling **step** off the F (gap-fill after the
  leaps).
- **Bars 3–4 (antecedent close)**: **sequence** the motif a step lower, steer the
  harmony `i – iv – V` and end **open on V (A major chord)** — a half cadence, the
  question.
- **Bars 5–6**: restate the opening (so the phrases rhyme), but now climb higher —
  the **climax** is a high G on the downbeat of bar 6, an **appoggiatura** leaning
  onto F over the tonic. The sigh.
- **Bars 7–8 (consequent close)**: descend by step, `iv – V7 – i`, a **suspension
  (4–3)** over the V, land on D — a perfect authentic cadence. Home.

Same six-note motif the whole way; one peak; one suspension at the climax; a
question that gets answered. That's a complete, beautiful little piece — and
nothing in it is random.
