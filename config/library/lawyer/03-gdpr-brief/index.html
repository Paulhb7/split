SECTION 01

Your role, and why the GDPR applies to all of it

Brume decides why and how personal data is processed — which features need which data, which vendors touch it, how long it lives. That makes Brume the controller Art. 4(7). The cloud host, the analytics SDK, the push-notification service: those are processors Art. 4(8), acting on your instructions.

The regulation applies because you are established in the EU — a French SAS processing personal data in the context of that establishment Art. 3(1). It applies to the whole ecosystem as one thing: the mobile app, the device firmware, the cloud backend, the support desk. There is no “the app is compliant but the backend is someone else’s problem” — the purposes are yours, so the obligations are yours.

The quiet clause that shapes everything else is accountability Art. 5(2): you must not only comply, you must be able to demonstrate compliance. In practice that means the deliverables of this brief are mostly documents — a processing record, a lawful-basis map, signed processor agreements, a retention schedule, an assessment or two. Compliance you cannot show does not exist, as far as a regulator is concerned.

What this means for you

You cannot outsource responsibility. If the analytics SDK you embedded leaks data, the CNIL’s first letter goes to you, not to the SDK vendor.

Budget for paperwork as a product cost: the proof is the compliance.

SECTION 02

What counts as personal data here — more than you think

Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable person Art. 4(1). For Brume, the honest inventory looks like this:

  • Account data — name, email, password hash, locale, country.
  • Device identifiers — serial number, MAC address, BLE pairing identifiers. Once a device is paired to an account, its identifiers point to a person.
  • Sensor telemetry — air-quality readings are environmental data in isolation, but linked to an account and a home they describe a person’s environment and habits. Linked telemetry is personal data.
  • Usage analytics — screens viewed, features used, session times, tied to a user or device ID.
  • Technical exhaust — IP addresses, crash logs, server logs, support tickets. Crash logs routinely embed identifiers.

The trap is the word “anonymous”. If you keep a key that can link a record back to a person — a user ID, a device ID, a hashed email — the data is pseudonymised, not anonymised, and pseudonymised data is squarely personal data Recital 26. True anonymisation is irreversible, and it is harder than it sounds.

One boundary worth guarding: air-quality data is not a special category Art. 9. But the day a product manager pitches “asthma insights” or “sleep quality scores”, you may cross into health data, and the rules harden considerably. Flag that in the roadmap review before it ships, not after.

What this means for you

“Our telemetry is anonymous” is usually wrong. If any table in your warehouse can join it back to an account, it is personal data and everything in this brief applies to it.

Keep a one-page data inventory. It becomes your Art. 30 record almost for free.

SECTION 03

Lawful bases — one per purpose, chosen before launch

Every processing purpose needs exactly one lawful basis from Art. 6(1), picked deliberately and written down. You do not get to swap bases later when the first one becomes inconvenient — that violates fairness and transparency Art. 5(1)(a). The sensible map for Brume:

PurposeBasisNotes
Account creation, device pairing, core app features, firmware updates Contract
Art. 6(1)(b)		 Necessary to deliver the service the user signed up for. “Necessary” is read strictly — nice-to-have data does not ride along.
Security, fraud prevention, abuse detection, service logs Legitimate interests
Art. 6(1)(f)		 Do the three-part test — purpose, necessity, balancing — and keep the written assessment (Recital 47).
Detailed product analytics via third-party SDKs Consent
Art. 6(1)(a)		 In France the tracker layer independently requires consent (see section 09), so consent is the practical basis. A narrow exempt audience-measurement setup is possible under strict CNIL conditions.
Marketing emails and push campaigns Consent
Art. 6(1)(a)		 Must meet Art. 7: freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, and as easy to withdraw as to give (Art. 7(3)).
Invoicing, tax and accounting records Legal obligation
Art. 6(1)(c)		 French commercial and tax law sets the retention here, not your preferences.

Two disciplines make this map real. First, purpose limitation Art. 5(1)(b): data collected to pair a device is not free raw material for an advertising model. Second, wherever you claim legitimate interests, write the balancing test down — the CNIL will ask for it, and “we figured it was fine” is not a balancing test.

What this means for you

One purpose, one basis, in writing. A two-column spreadsheet — purpose, basis — drafted this week is the spine of your entire compliance file.

Anything resting on consent must be able to switch off cleanly when consent is refused or withdrawn. Design the off-switch first.

SECTION 04

Telling people — the privacy notice is a product surface

Transparency is a design requirement, not a legal-page requirement. Information must be concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible, in clear and plain language Art. 12(1) — the regulation itself bans legalese here.

When you collect data from the user, Art. 13 lists what they must be told: who you are, each purpose and its lawful basis, your legitimate interests where you claim them, who receives the data, whether it leaves the EU, how long you keep it, the full list of their rights, and their right to complain to the CNIL. If a field is contractually required, say so, and say what happens if they decline.

How to ship it well

  • Layer it. A short in-app summary at signup — five lines, honest — linking to the full notice. Both count together.
  • Put it where the data is collected: signup, device pairing, and the analytics consent screen each carry their own line of context.
  • Keep it in sync with the build. The notice describes what the app actually does this release, not what the last release did.
What this means for you

Write the notice like onboarding copy. Same plain voice, same care. A notice your support team can read aloud without wincing is roughly the legal standard.

Version it in git next to the app. When a release changes data flows, the notice changes in the same pull request.

SECTION 05

Privacy by design and by default — duties at the whiteboard

Art. 25 puts obligations at the moment you design the system, not the moment you get audited. Two distinct duties:

  • By design Art. 25(1) — build technical and organisational measures (pseudonymisation, minimisation) into the processing itself, from the start.
  • By default Art. 25(2) — out of the box, the product processes only what is necessary. The most protective setting is the shipped setting.

What that looks like for Brume, concretely

  • Analytics SDKs off by default, activated only after consent — not “fires on first launch, opt-out in settings”.
  • Minimisation Art. 5(1)(c) as a schema rule: every field in the signup form and every event in the telemetry pipeline earns its place, or it goes.
  • Separate identity from telemetry where you can: sensor readings keyed to a device ID, the device-to-account join held separately and access-controlled.
  • Do not log raw BLE/MAC identifiers into analytics events; treat hardware identifiers as personal data plumbing, not as free labels.
  • Decide retention at schema-design time — a table with no deletion story is a finding waiting to be written.
What this means for you

The cheapest compliance is data you never collected. Every field you drop removes cost in security, retention, rights handling and breach exposure — simultaneously.

Add one line to your definition of done: “new data collected? then basis, notice, retention — named in the PR.”

SECTION 06

Your vendors — processor agreements and the transfer map

Every service that touches personal data on your behalf needs a written data processing agreement Art. 28(3). For a typical stack that means: the cloud host, the analytics provider, crash reporting, push notifications, the email service, the support-desk tool. The mandatory clauses are enumerated in the article itself — the processor acts only on your documented instructions, guarantees confidentiality and security, seeks approval for sub-processors, assists with user rights and breaches, and deletes or returns the data at the end.

Most serious vendors ship a standard DPA. Your job is not to draft one from scratch; it is to collect them, read the sub-processor lists, and keep a register — which vendor, what data, what purpose, signed when.

When data leaves the EEA

Transfers outside the EEA need a Chapter V mechanism Arts. 44–49: an adequacy decision for the destination country Art. 45, or appropriate safeguards — most commonly the Standard Contractual Clauses Art. 46(2)(c), paired with a documented transfer risk assessment. Many US-based SaaS vendors rely on adequacy-framework certifications or SCCs; frameworks evolve, so record which mechanism each vendor relies on today and check it when contracts renew. If a vendor cannot tell you where the data is processed, that is your answer about the vendor.

What this means for you

Your compliance is now a supply chain. One page: vendor · data · purpose · region · transfer mechanism · DPA date. Maintained, this answers half of any CNIL questionnaire.

Prefer EU hosting regions where the vendor offers them — it deletes the whole transfer analysis for that flow.

SECTION 07

Retention — every table needs an expiry date

Storage limitation Art. 5(1)(e): keep personal data in identifiable form no longer than the purpose requires. “We keep everything, storage is cheap” is a compliance violation stated as an architecture principle.

A defensible starting schedule for Brume — adjust with counsel:

DataKeep whileThen
Account & pairing data Account is active Delete on closure; archive the minimum needed for legal claims, per statutory limitation periods
Linked sensor telemetry As long as the feature shows it to the user Aggregate or truly anonymise; drop the account link
Analytics events A defined window (commonly ~13–25 months in CNIL guidance for measurement data) Delete or anonymise
Server & security logs Months, not years (6–12 months is the commonly cited CNIL order of magnitude) Delete
Prospect / marketing data ~3 years from last contact (the CNIL’s long-standing benchmark) Delete or re-ask
Invoices & accounting Statutory commercial/tax periods These override your preferences — legal obligation basis

Two rules make the schedule real. Deletion must be automated — a policy no cron job enforces is a declaration of intent. And “anonymised” only exits the GDPR if it is irreversible Recital 26; aggregation with the keys thrown away qualifies, hashing an email does not.

What this means for you

Write the number down, then wire the job. For each table: a retention period, a deletion mechanism, an owner. Three columns, done in an afternoon, audited in minutes.

SECTION 08

User rights — build the workflow before the first email

EU users hold a bundle of rights you must honour within one month, extendable by two more for complex cases if you explain why Art. 12(3), and free of charge in the normal case Art. 12(5):

  • Access Art. 15 — a copy of their data plus the context: purposes, recipients, retention, source.
  • Rectification Art. 16 — fix what is wrong.
  • Erasure Art. 17 — delete, subject to real exceptions (legal obligations, defence of legal claims). Invoices survive; telemetry does not.
  • Restriction Art. 18 — freeze processing while a dispute is resolved.
  • Portability Art. 20 — data they provided, in a structured, machine-readable format. A JSON export honestly meets this.
  • Objection Art. 21 — absolute for direct marketing; for legitimate-interests processing you must stop unless you show compelling grounds.

Verify identity proportionately — if the request comes from the logged-in account, that is normally enough; demand ID documents only when doubt is real Art. 12(6). The strongest move is self-service: in-app “export my data” and “delete my account” buttons resolve most requests without a human, on time, with an audit trail.

What this means for you

Make “delete my account” actually cascade — production rows, analytics profiles, processor copies, and a stated backup-expiry story.

Log every request and its clock. A large share of CNIL complaints begin with one ignored email.

SECTION 09

Cookies and trackers — a second law, and the CNIL’s favourite audit

Trackers are governed by a rule alongside the GDPR: the ePrivacy directive’s terminal-equipment rule Dir. 2002/58/EC, Art. 5(3), transposed in France as Art. 82, Loi Informatique et Libertés. It protects the device, so it covers far more than browser cookies: mobile SDK identifiers, advertising IDs, local storage, fingerprinting — anything read from or written to the phone that is not strictly necessary for the service.

The CNIL’s published position, stable for years, comes down to:

  • Consent before firing. Non-essential trackers — analytics SDKs included — stay silent until the user consents. Consent must meet Art. 4(11) and Art. 7: no pre-ticked boxes, no consent-by-scrolling.
  • Refusing must be as easy as accepting. A first-layer “refuse all” as prominent as “accept all”. Dark patterns are the thing the CNIL fines.
  • Keep proof of who consented to what, when, via which screen.
  • Strictly necessary is exempt — session state, security, the consent choice itself.
  • A narrow audience-measurement exemption exists under CNIL conditions (own-purpose statistics only, no cross-site/app reuse, limited lifetimes). Most default SDK configurations do not qualify — check before assuming.

For a mobile app the equivalent of a cookie banner is the consent screen before the first SDK call. Platform permission dialogs (Bluetooth, location) are not GDPR consent — they are OS gates. If the app ever collects fine location for BLE-adjacent features, that is its own purpose with its own basis and its own line in the notice.

What this means for you

Audit the SDKs before writing the notice. Instrument a clean build and watch what fires on first launch — the notice must describe what the build does, not the reverse.

Every third-party SDK is both a tracker question (this section) and a processor question (section 06). Ask both every time.

SECTION 10

The DPIA question — you probably need to answer it in writing

A Data Protection Impact Assessment is mandatory when processing is “likely to result in a high risk” to people Art. 35(1). The EDPB’s working method lists nine risk criteria — systematic monitoring, data processed at large scale, innovative technology, matching datasets, vulnerable subjects, and so on — with a rule of thumb: two or more criteria met, run the DPIA.

Be honest about Brume’s profile: a connected device continuously observing the home environment is plausibly systematic monitoring plus innovative technology, and large scale arrives with growth. The CNIL also publishes a list of processing types where a DPIA is required outright — several patterns on it (large-scale location data, profiling, certain connected-object scenarios) sit close to this product category.

The practical move is cheap: run a documented threshold assessment now — one page walking the nine criteria against your actual data flows. If it concludes “no DPIA needed”, you hold the proof of a reasoned decision. If it triggers, the DPIA itself has a fixed shape Art. 35(7): describe the processing, justify necessity and proportionality, assess the risks, list the mitigations. The CNIL publishes free guides and an open-source PIA tool for exactly this. If residual risk stays high after mitigations, you must consult the CNIL before processing Art. 36 — in practice, a signal to redesign until it does not.

What this means for you

The threshold assessment is one page; skipping it is indefensible. Write it even if the conclusion is “not required”, and date it.

Redo it when the product changes shape — new sensor, new SDK, health-adjacent features, a B2B fleet offering.

SECTION 11

Living with the CNIL — the French specifics

With your main establishment in France, the CNIL is your lead supervisory authority under the one-stop-shop Art. 56 — one regulator fronting your EU-wide processing. Useful specifics:

  • DPO Art. 37 — mandatory only in defined cases, notably where core activities involve regular and systematic monitoring at large scale. A growing fleet of always-on home devices trends toward that test, so many device companies designate early. The role can be part-time or external; the designation is filed with the CNIL online. Either way, name an internal privacy owner now.
  • Breaches — notify the CNIL within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to result in risk Art. 33(1); document every breach internally, notified or not Art. 33(5); tell affected users directly when the risk is high Art. 34. Write the runbook before the incident: who decides, who drafts, who files.
  • Enforcement — fines reach €10 M / 2 % of worldwide turnover for one tier of violations Art. 83(4) and €20 M / 4 % for the other Art. 83(5). The CNIL’s everyday instruments matter more at your size: formal notices (mises en demeure, sometimes public) and a simplified sanction procedure for routine cases. Cookie-consent design and ignored rights requests are recurring themes.
  • Free assets — the CNIL publishes referentials, model records, retention benchmarks, developer guides and the PIA tool, in French. For a French startup this is the cheapest competent help available; use it before paying anyone.
What this means for you

The CNIL rewards evidence. In a control, the folder — record, basis map, DPAs, assessments, breach log — is your defence. Build the folder in quiet times.

Subscribe someone to the CNIL’s publications; guidance moves and this brief is deliberately conservative.
