What ACVR1 does

ACVR1 (also ALK2) is a type-I BMP receptor — a membrane serine/threonine kinase in the TGF-β superfamily. When a bone-morphogenetic-protein ligand binds, type-II receptors phosphorylate ACVR1's GS domain; ACVR1 then phosphorylates SMAD1/5/8, and that signal instructs cells to build cartilage and bone.

Why R206H makes it hyperactive

the lesion

Arginine 206 sits at the very end of the GS domain, at its junction with the kinase. In the resting receptor this region — together with bound FKBP12 — acts as a clamp that keeps the kinase off until a ligand arrives.

Swapping Arg206→His weakens FKBP12 binding, so the clamp slips: ACVR1 signals without ligand — "leaky" activation. Worse, the mutant is neofunctional — Activin A, normally an ACVR1 antagonist, becomes an agonist. The receptor is stuck driving bone formation in muscle, tendon and ligament.

Why it's druggable

the pocket

ACVR1 is a kinase, and kinases run on ATP. The ATP-binding pocket — the cleft between the two lobes where the inhibitor sits in this structure — is a classic small-molecule target: block it and the mutant receptor can't phosphorylate SMADs, even while stuck "on".

The bound molecule here is dorsomorphin (component TAK), the tool compound that first revealed this pocket as an ALK2 target and seeded the clinical inhibitors.

The disease, in brief

FOP is ultra-rare — the IFOPA estimates roughly 1 in a million, about 900 confirmed cases worldwide. It is autosomal dominant, but most cases arise de novo. The tell at birth is malformed great toes; from childhood, episodic flares — often after trauma, falls, or intramuscular injections — ossify soft tissue into a progressive "second skeleton."
