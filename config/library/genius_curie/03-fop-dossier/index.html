Fibrodysplasia Ossificans Progressiva

A single gene mistake turns the body’s own repair signal into a builder of bone. Muscle, tendon and ligament are quietly replaced by a second skeleton — and the surgeon’s knife only makes it worse.

§1

The clinical picture

Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva announces itself before any bone has formed. Almost every child born with classic FOP has a short, inturned great toe — a monophalangic hallux with valgus deviation — present at birth and, in retrospect, the single most reliable early sign of the disease.24 It is frequently the only clue for years, and it is almost universally missed.

Then, usually within the first decade, the ossification begins. Episodes of painful, warm, tumour-like soft-tissue swelling — flare-ups — erupt, often after minor trauma, an intramuscular injection, a viral illness, muscular over-stretching, fatigue or a fall; some arise with no trigger at all.14 What looks like an inflammatory mass is the opening act of heterotopic ossification (HO): the flare resolves not by healing back to muscle, but by laying down qualitatively normal bone where bone should never be — through muscle, fascia, tendon, ligament and aponeurosis.1

The march of new bone follows a strikingly consistent anatomical order — the same sequence in which the embryonic skeleton once formed:24

  • Axial before appendicular — the neck, spine and shoulder girdle ossify before the limbs.
  • Cranial before caudal — upper body before lower.
  • Proximal before distal, and dorsal before ventral.

Over years, a second skeleton bridges the joints. Ribbons and plates of heterotopic bone lock the spine, fuse the jaw, immobilise the shoulders and hips, and progressively imprison the patient — the reason FOP has long carried the lay name “stone-man syndrome.” Most patients are confined to a wheelchair by their thirties, and thoracic insufficiency from a rigid chest wall is the leading cause of premature death.14

⚠ The iatrogenic catastrophe

The cruelest feature of FOP is that trauma to soft tissue provokes bone. A diagnostic biopsy of a flare-up can trigger explosive, permanent heterotopic ossification and must never be performed; the same is true of intramuscular injections (including many routine vaccines given IM), dental blocks, and elective surgery to “remove” existing HO — excised bone reliably regrows, often worse.414 The single most protective act a clinician can perform is to recognise the toe, make the diagnosis on sight, and not cut.

§2

The molecular mechanism

FOP is a disease of a single, hyperactive receptor. In ~97% of people with classic FOP the cause is one recurrent heterozygous point mutation — c.617G>A in ACVR1, replacing arginine 206 with histidine (R206H) — making it one of the most specific genotype–phenotype relationships in all of medicine.3

ACVR1 (also called ALK2; UniProt Q04771, 509 aa) encodes a type I BMP receptor serine/threonine kinase.6 Residue 206 sits in its GS domain (the glycine–serine-rich regulatory segment, residues 178–207), the switch that sits just N-terminal to the kinase domain (208–502) and keeps the receptor silent until it is legitimately activated.67 In the healthy receptor the GS domain is clamped by the inhibitory protein FKBP12; the R206H substitution destabilises that clamp, loosening the autoinhibited state.7

But the decisive lesion is not simply “more signalling.” In 2015 two groups showed that R206H confers a neofunction: the mutant receptor gains the ability to respond to Activin A — a ligand that, at the wild-type receptor, binds but produces no signal (a non-signalling complex).8 In FOP cells, Activin A — abundant at sites of injury and inflammation — now drives the mutant ACVR1 to fire the canonical BMP pathway, phosphorylating SMAD1/5/8 and transcribing an osteochondrogenic program in tissue-resident fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs).815 This is the molecular reason a flare-up ends in bone, and why injury — which floods tissue with Activin A — is such a potent trigger. Blocking Activin A abolishes HO in FOP mouse models, and that single insight defines an entire therapeutic axis.15

The kinase domain of ALK2 has been solved crystallographically — e.g. PDB 3H9R, the ALK2 kinase in complex with FKBP12 and the inhibitor dorsomorphin (X-ray, 2.35 Å) — and these structures explain why FOP mutations misbehave: they break the interactions that stabilise the inactive kinase and displace FKBP12, tipping the receptor toward a signalling-competent conformation.7 That same ATP-binding cleft is the pocket every small-molecule ALK2 inhibitor in the pipeline is built to occupy.

§3

Diagnosis & the imaging course

FOP is a clinical diagnosis made on sight — and it can be made without a single invasive test.

The pairing is essentially pathognomonic: congenital great-toe malformation plus soft-tissue swellings that mature into heterotopic bone.414 A plain radiograph of the feet shows the short, monophalangic hallux; imaging of an active region later shows sheets and columns of ectopic bone in soft tissue. Genetic confirmation — sequencing ACVR1 for the c.617G>A (R206H) variant — is definitive and, crucially, non-invasive.314

Do Recognise it early

  • Examine the great toes of any child with unexplained soft-tissue lumps.
  • Confirm with ACVR1 R206H sequencing — a blood test, not a biopsy.
  • Stage the skeleton with low-dose whole-body CT for HO burden where needed.10

Never Confirm by cutting

  • No diagnostic biopsy of a flare — it seeds new bone.
  • No intramuscular injections; use SC/oral routes and avoid IM vaccines where possible.14
  • No surgical excision of HO to “free” a joint — it regrows.

The tragedy of misdiagnosis is well documented: because the swellings mimic aggressive fibromatosis, sarcoma or infection, historical series report that a large majority of patients were misdiagnosed — often only after a biopsy or excision had already triggered irreversible ossification.5 The whole point of knowing the toe sign is to stop that first, catastrophic cut.

§4

Management today — and what not to do

There is no cure. Care is protective, anticipatory, and organised around one principle: do not provoke bone.

Flare management. International expert guidance (a modified-Delphi physician consensus, and updated 2025 medical guidelines) recommends a short, high-dose corticosteroid course — approximately 1–2 mg/kg/day of prednisone (max ~100 mg) — started within 24 hours of a flare involving a major joint, and continued for a few days; it may also be used prophylactically after unavoidable trauma or before non-avoidable surgery.1014 This blunts the inflammatory flare; it does not reverse bone already formed.

Prevention is the therapy. Because injury is the trigger, day-to-day management is overwhelmingly about avoidance and protection:1014

  • Fall prevention — home safety, mobility aids, and protective headgear; prompt medical attention (and consideration of prophylactic steroids) after any fall.
  • Avoid IM injections and biopsies entirely; give vaccines subcutaneously where an alternative exists; use extreme caution with dental blocks.
  • Anaesthesia planning — awake fibre-optic intubation and careful positioning for the fused jaw and rigid neck/chest; no elective surgery to remove HO.
  • Respiratory and dental surveillance, physiotherapy that never forces a stiff joint, and multidisciplinary care through an FOP centre.
Patient & clinician resource

The International FOP Association (IFOPA) maintains the FOP registry, treatment guidelines, and a directory of expert centres; it is the definitive first stop for a newly suspected or confirmed case.

The therapeutic landscape · July 2026

For two centuries FOP had no treatment. In the last three years it has acquired its first approved drug and a genuine pipeline — organised around two rational targets: the retinoid pathway, the Activin A ligand, and the ALK2 kinase itself.

§6

The repurposing angle

This is where an orphan disease borrows a drug that has already paid its dues in safety. Saracatinib (AZD0530) was developed by AstraZeneca as an oral Src-family / Abl kinase inhibitor and taken through numerous phase I–II oncology trials — hormone-receptor-negative breast cancer, recurrent osteosarcoma, pancreatic and colorectal cancer — where it was ultimately shelved for lack of efficacy.17

But a shelved oncology drug carries something precious: a documented human safety and pharmacokinetic profile. When kinase-inhibitor screens flagged saracatinib as a potent inhibitor of the ALK2/ACVR1 kinase — the exact receptor mutated in FOP — and it was shown to prevent heterotopic ossification in FOP mouse models, the case for repurposing was immediate: skip years of first-in-human safety work and go straight to a proof-of-concept efficacy trial.1718 That trial is STOPFOP (NCT04307953), a European academic phase 2 rescued from the brink of collapse by IFOPA patient-community funding — a rare-disease story in miniature.18

Curie’s discipline — a lead is not a result

Repurposing is the fastest ethical route to a rare-disease therapy, but it earns no free pass on efficacy. The logic that a drug hits the right kinase gets it into the clinic; it does not get it through. FALKON — a purpose-built ALK2 inhibitor that failed its endpoint — is the cautionary case:16 right target, wrong outcome. The honest reading of saracatinib in July 2026 is a completed trial with results presented but not yet fully published; the definitive verdict waits on the peer-reviewed data, not the mechanism.

§7

State of the science — July 2026

FOP is, mechanistically, one of the best-understood rare diseases in existence — a single recurrent mutation, a single hyperactive receptor, one neofunction, one traceable signalling axis to bone. That clarity is exactly why it has become a proving ground for rational, targeted therapy.

Three years on from the first-ever approval (palovarotene, 2023), the field now has two more filings under FDA priority review — Regeneron’s Activin-A antibody garetosmab (action date August 2026) and Incyte/Mirum’s oral ALK2 inhibitor zilurgisertib (PDUFA September 2026)1113 — attacking the pathway at two different points. Should either or both clear, FOP would move within a single decade from untreatable to a disease with multiple mechanistically distinct options.

The honesty that this dossier owes its reader: none of these agents reverses bone already laid down — they aim to prevent the next flare’s ossification. FALKON’s failure16 is a reminder that inhibiting the right kinase is necessary, not sufficient. And the deepest ambition — gene-level correction of the R206H allele, or durable Activin-A blockade — remains preclinical.15 The trajectory is genuinely, unusually hopeful; the work is not finished. For a disease that had nothing to offer for two hundred years, that is a remarkable place to stand — and precisely the kind of frontier where the next repurposing insight, or the next antibody, changes a life.
