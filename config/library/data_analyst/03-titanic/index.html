About this exhibit curator’s brief — click to unfold

Why

A showcase analysis of the classic Titanic passenger dataset: who survived the night of 14–15 April 1912, and what the manifest itself says about why — class, sex, age, fare and family, quantified.

Context

The data is the standard 891-passenger teaching extract of the Titanic manifest (not the full passenger list, and no crew). All 891 rows are embedded in this file; every figure recomputes from them, live.

How to use

Read top to bottom like a museum panel. Use the booking-office buttons to restrict every plate to a class, a sex, or both; hover any bar, ribbon or fare tick for the exact count.

What was assumed

177 passengers with no recorded age are excluded from age-based figures only. “Child” means under 16. Family size = siblings/spouses + parents/children + self. Fares are kept in pounds as recorded; 15 zero-fare tickets plot at the left edge.

Findings

Curator’s Notes

I

Class was the strongest single line in the ledger.

First class survived at 63.0%, second at 47.3%, third at 24.2% — and the gradient holds within each sex: women fell from 96.8% in first to 92.1% in second to 50.0% in third; men from 36.9% to 15.7% to 13.5%. The purse tells the same story: the median fare of the saved was £26.0, against £10.5 for the lost.

II

“Women and children first” is measurable — and class-filtered.

Women survived at 74.2% against 18.9% for men — 3.9× the rate. Children under 16 survived at 59.0% against 17.4% for adult men. Yet the order passed through the decks unevenly: every second-class child in the sample lived (19 of 19), while in third class fewer than half did (25 of 58, 43.1%). A third-class woman’s odds (50.0%) were only just above a first-class man’s (36.9%).

III

A small family helped; a large one was a liability.

Survival climbs with company — 30.4% alone, 55.3% in pairs, 72.4% in parties of four — then collapses to 20.0%, 13.6% and 16.0% for parties of five, six and seven-plus. The large parties were overwhelmingly third-class (54 of 62 passengers), so scale and steerage compounded: harder to gather, and starting further from the boats.

Source: the classic 891-passenger Titanic teaching extract (Data Science Dojo mirror of the canonical training split), embedded in this file in full. It covers passengers of record only — neither the complete manifest nor the crew — so rates describe this sample, not the whole ship. Method: 177 unrecorded ages excluded from age-based figures only; “child” = under 16; family size = SibSp + Parch + self; fares in pounds as recorded, 15 zero-fare tickets plotted at the left edge of Plate III.
