How to read infrared, honestly

None of the colours in this gallery are what your eye would see. Webb records infrared — light with wavelengths stretched past red, invisible to us, but exactly where cool dust glows, where newborn stars leak through their cocoons, and where the expansion of the Universe delivers the light of the first galaxies. To make a picture, each infrared exposure is assigned a visible colour: the shortest wavelengths captured become blue, the longest become red. The order of the rainbow is preserved; it is a translation, not a fiction.

So a rough grammar for every plate below: blue tends to be starlight; orange and red, warm dust; yellow-green, in several images, a family of soot-like molecules (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons) that trace where clouds are being cooked by young stars. And the six sharp spikes on bright stars are not real — they are the signature of Webb's hexagonal mirrors, worn by any point of light bright enough.

Two cameras took these. NIRCam works in the near-infrared (0.6–5 µm) — sharp, star-rich, good at peering through dust. MIRI works in the mid-infrared (5–28 µm) — it sees the dust itself glowing, and the oldest, most redshifted light. The ruler below is to scale. The white sliver is everything your eye can do.

Plate I

Neptune, wearing auroras

Object
Neptune — auroral emission, imaged for the first time
Distance
About 4 light-hours (≈4.3 billion km)
Instrument
Webb NIRSpec (auroras), over Hubble WFC3 (planet)
Released
26 March 2025

Two portraits of the same planet. On the left, Hubble's view alone; on the right, the same view with Webb's detection laid over it. The cyan patches are auroras — charged particles caught by Neptune's magnetic field and driven into the upper atmosphere — and no one had ever photographed them before. Voyager 2 saw hints during its 1989 flyby. Confirmation took another thirty-six years.

What Webb's spectrograph actually detected is the glow of the trihydrogen cation, H3+ — the standard chemical fingerprint of an aurora on a giant planet. Neptune was the last of the four giants holding out. Note where the light sits: mid-latitudes, not the poles, because Neptune's magnetic field is tilted 47 degrees away from its spin axis. On Neptune, the compass and the globe barely speak.

Look closer

The largest cyan patch sits at the latitude where Earth keeps its temperate zones — polar lights, nowhere near a pole.

Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI, Heidi Hammel (AURA), Henrik Melin (Northumbria University), Leigh Fletcher (University of Leicester), Stefanie Milam (NASA-GSFC) · Original release ↗

Plate II

Two planetary systems, edge-on

Objects
Protoplanetary discs Tau 042021 and Oph 163131
Distance
≈450 and ≈480 light-years (Taurus; Ophiuchus)
Instruments
NIRCam + MIRI, with Hubble and ALMA context
Released
3 April 2026

Two planetary systems under construction, seen exactly edge-on — a lucky geometry. The disc itself is the dark lane across the middle of each figure; the newborn star is buried inside it, hidden by its own building material. What you see is starlight escaping above and below the dust, lighting the disc's surfaces like a road at night.

The geometry is the science: seen from the side, the disc reveals how its dust settles and layers — and where the dust settles decides where planets can form. In Oph 163131, radio observations with ALMA show a gap in the disc, the kind of lane a young planet clears along its orbit. To be precise: a gap is a hint, not a planet. No one has confirmed what's in there yet.

Look closer

The thin dark midplane between the bright lobes. That line is a future planetary system in cross-section — ours likely looked just like it, 4.6 billion years ago.

Credit: ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, ESA/Hubble, ALMA (ESO/NAOJ/NRAO), G. Duchêne, M. Villenave · Original release ↗

Plate III

Herbig-Haro 49/50, the Cosmic Tornado

Object
A protostellar outflow — shocked gas lit by a newborn star's jet
Distance
≈630 light-years (Chamaeleon)
Instruments
NIRCam + MIRI
Released
24 March 2025

Not a tornado — a shockwave. A jet fired from a newborn star is ramming into slower gas, and these glowing arcs are the collision, drawn out over light-years. The likely culprit, a protostar called Cederblad 110 IRS4, sits about 1.5 light-years off the lower edge of the frame. Jets like this are how forming stars shed spin; without them, stars would struggle to finish forming at all.

The fuzzy object at the funnel's tip puzzled astronomers for nearly twenty years — Spitzer imaged it in 2006 and couldn't resolve it. Webb settled it: a face-on spiral galaxy, millions of light-years beyond, sitting on the same line of sight by pure chance. Astronomy's oldest trap, photographed: two things on one sightline, at absurdly different distances.

Look closer

The very tip of the funnel — a whole spiral galaxy, photobombing from a distance millions of times greater than the tornado's.

Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI · Original release ↗

Plate IV

A nursery in Orion, every stage at once

Object
Orion Molecular Cloud 2 (OMC-2), a star-forming filament
Distance
1,280 light-years (Orion)
Instrument
NIRCam
Released
5 June 2026

One filament of the Orion cloud, and on it, stars at every stage of being born: some still buried in the collapsing gas that feeds them, some already burning their surroundings clear. Newborn stars fire jets from their poles, and where a jet strikes the cloud, the gas lights up in bright ridges — shock fronts, the nursery's alarm bells.

Read it with the colour grammar from the entrance: orange-brown is warm dust; the yellow-green sheen is soot-like molecules fluorescing under young starlight; the blue-cyan haze is ordinary starlight scattering off fine grains. You rarely get the whole sequence — deeply embedded protostar to nearly finished star — in a single field this close. That is what makes this filament a textbook, not just a picture.

Look closer

Pick a bright, sharp-edged ridge and trace it back. Every jet points to a protostar you cannot see directly — the outflows betray the hidden.

Credit: ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, T. Megeath, M. Zamani (ESA/Webb); acknowledgement: M. H. Özsaraç · Original release ↗

Plate V

The Red Spider Nebula, a star ending politely

Object
NGC 6537, a planetary nebula — a dying Sun-like star
Distance
A few thousand light-years (Sagittarius) — genuinely uncertain; the release declines to give a figure, and so will I
Instrument
NIRCam
Released
28 October 2025

A star roughly like the Sun, at the end. It has shed its outer layers into space; the exposed core — around 160,000 kelvin at the surface — now lights the wreckage from inside. The blue-rimmed lobes are molecular hydrogen, each stretching about three light-years. The red glow around the centre is a shroud of hot dust, and inside it Webb found something Hubble never saw: a disc around the dying star.

The purple S threading the waist is ionised iron, traced where a fast jet whips through shells thrown off earlier. An S like that, and an hourglass figure like this, usually mean a second star is stirring the pot — suspected here, not yet seen. Two humbling numbers: this phase lasts only tens of thousands of years, a blink in stellar time; and the Sun writes its own version in about five billion.

Look closer

The purple S-curve through the middle — a precessing jet signing its name in iron.

Credit: ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, J. H. Kastner (Rochester Institute of Technology) · Original release ↗

Plate VI

The Cat's Paw, one toe

Object
A single "toe bean" of NGC 6334, a massive star-forming region
Distance
≈4,000 light-years (Scorpius)
Instrument
NIRCam
Released
10 July 2025 — Webb's third-anniversary image

One toe of a much larger paw-print of star formation. The massive newborn stars — the yellowish ones wearing diffraction spikes — are carving this cavity from the inside; the blue glow is their light filling the hollow. Red-orange pockets mark where the next generation of massive stars is forming right now. The brown is dust so dense that even Webb cannot see through it.

Massive stars edit their own birthplaces. Their radiation and winds disperse the cloud that made them, which sets a timer on how many more stars that cloud can make — creation and eviction in the same gesture. This frame catches the eviction in progress, which is why NASA chose it to mark Webb's third year of science.

Look closer

Top centre: a tiered, circular hollow the release nicknames the Opera House — each level carved by the stars seated below it.

Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI · Original release ↗

Plate VII

Messier 77, feeding

Object
The Squid Galaxy — a barred spiral with an active nucleus
Distance
45 million light-years (Cetus)
Instrument
MIRI
Released
7 May 2026

A spiral galaxy with its lights switched to the mid-infrared — so what you are seeing is not its stars but its dust, drawn as a skeleton. The blinding point at the centre is the reason this galaxy is famous: an active nucleus, a black hole of about eight million solar masses, feeding. The matter falling toward it glows brighter than entire spiral arms.

Around it runs a starburst ring more than 6,000 light-years across, making stars at a furious rate; each orange bubble along it is a cluster of newborn stars wrapped in its own dust cocoon. M77 is one of the nearest galaxies with a feeding supermassive black hole — the reference specimen for how a nucleus and its host live together. The six-pointed star on the core is the telescope's own optics; a point that bright cannot help wearing it.

Look closer

Any single orange bubble on the ring — each one is a whole star cluster, counted here as a dot.

Credit: ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, A. Leroy · Original release ↗

Plate VIII

Two clusters, mid-collision

Object
MACS J0553.4−3342, two merging galaxy clusters
Distance
≈4 billion light-years (Columba)
Instrument
NIRCam
Released
3 July 2026 — the day this gallery was hung

Two clusters of galaxies — each a swarm of hundreds, each anchored by a giant elliptical — that have already passed through each other. They are now more than a million light-years apart and slowing; gravity will pull them back for another pass. The space between them is filled with gas heated by the crash until it shines in X-rays — a part of the picture Webb cannot see, and says nothing about.

Collisions like this are the most energetic events since the Big Bang, and they are a laboratory for dark matter: in a crash, the galaxies, the hot gas, and the invisible mass separate, and their separation can be measured. Meanwhile the combined mass works as a lens — the orange arcs are galaxies far behind the wreck, their light bent and stretched on the way to us, some of it from less than a billion years after the beginning.

Look closer

The left side of the frame: three separate orange smears that are the same background galaxy, delivered three times by the lens.

Credit: ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, S. Fujimoto · Original release ↗

Plate IX

The celebrity cluster, used as a telescope

Object
MACS J1149.5+2223, over 300 confirmed galaxies in one cluster
Distance
≈5 billion light-years (Leo)
Instrument
NIRCam, 0.9–4.44 µm
Released
22 January 2026

More than three hundred galaxies bound in one cluster — and the cluster itself used as an instrument. Its mass curves spacetime; light from galaxies far behind bends around it and arrives magnified, smeared into arcs. Astronomy gets a free telescope upgrade, paid for in distortion.

The field has earned its nickname. This is where the lensed supernova Refsdal appeared as multiple images on the sky, and the pink, jellyfish-shaped streak is the warped galaxy where astronomers found a blue supergiant that was, at its discovery, the most distant single star ever seen. Webb's revisit — the CANUCS programme — pushes past what Hubble's Frontier Fields could reach: through this lens it finds galaxies from the Universe's first 600 million years, some already feeding supermassive black holes. That last part was not supposed to happen so early; it is one of Webb's standing puzzles.

Look closer

The pink jellyfish. Its stretched shape is not the galaxy's own — it is the lens's signature, gravity redrawing the picture in transit.

Credit: ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, C. Willott (National Research Council Canada), R. Tripodi (INAF — Astronomical Observatory of Rome) · Original release ↗

Plate X

The deep field, deeper

Object
The Hubble Ultra Deep Field, re-photographed — the MIDIS survey
Distance
Up to 13+ billion years of lookback (Fornax)
Instruments
MIRI + NIRCam — nearly 100 hours, including Webb's longest single-filter extragalactic stare
Released
1 August 2025

The most famous patch of dark sky there is — Hubble's Ultra Deep Field — photographed again, this time in the mid-infrared, with nearly one hundred hours of exposure. Almost every mark in this frame is a galaxy: more than 2,500 sources in a patch of sky you could hide behind a grain of sand at arm's length. The white and green points carry light from the Universe's first billion years.

The red points are the finding. MIRI's long stare turned up hundreds of extremely red galaxies that earlier surveys could not classify — either choked with dust while forming stars furiously, or already full of old stars far earlier than galaxy-formation timelines comfortably allow. Both readings force a rewrite; deep fields are the census bureau of cosmology, and the census keeps coming back with surprises.

Look closer

Choose any patch that looks empty. Open the full-size image and look again. It isn't.

Credit: ESA/Webb, NASA & CSA, G. Östlin, P. G. Pérez-González, J. Melinder, the JADES Collaboration, the MIDIS collaboration, M. Zamani (ESA/Webb) · Original release ↗

Every colour on these walls arrived at a mirror a million miles from here as infrared light — some of it four hours old, some of it older than the Earth. The translation is honest. The distances are real. That is the whole exhibition.

On uncertainty

Distances to nebulae and clusters carry real error bars; figures here are as stated in each release, rounded honestly. Where the source declines to commit — the Red Spider's distance — this gallery declines too. Detection is not confirmation; hints are labelled as hints.
