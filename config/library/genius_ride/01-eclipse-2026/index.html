About this sheet — why it exists & what was assumed

Why

A single, verified reference for the total solar eclipse of 12 August 2026 — the path, the local timings, what each city will actually see, and how to watch it safely.

Context

This is the first totality over mainland Europe since 1999 and the first over Spain since 1905. It happens at low sun, near sunset in Iberia — an unusually unforgiving geometry that rewards planning.

The event

A shadow the size of a province, moving faster than sound

On the evening of 12 August 2026 the new Moon will pass squarely between the Earth and the Sun, and its inner shadow — the umbra, a cone of true darkness roughly 294 kilometres wide at the surface — will touch down in the Siberian Arctic, sweep across the polar cap, run down the east coast of Greenland, cross western Iceland at 17:44–17:50 UT, race south-southeast over the open Atlantic, and make landfall on the north coast of Spain at about 20:27 local time, dying at sunset just east of the Balearic Islands.

Inside that band, day becomes deep twilight: the corona — the Sun's outer atmosphere, a million times fainter than the disc — stands visible to the naked eye, the brighter planets come out, and the horizon glows sunset-orange in every direction at once. Outside the band, even one kilometre outside, none of this happens. A 99 % partial eclipse is not 99 % of the experience; it is a different, lesser event. That is the single most important fact on this sheet.

The occasion is historic three times over. It is the first total eclipse visible from mainland Europe since 11 August 1999; the first over Spain since 30 August 1905; and the first over Iceland since 30 June 1954 — an eclipse of the same Saros family, 126, four cycles earlier: the series' members return every 18.03 years, and every fourth visit finds nearly the same ground. And the Sun cooperates: Solar Cycle 25 passed its maximum in late 2024, so the corona should still wear its extravagant maximum form — petals and streamers all around the disc, not the thin equatorial wings of a quiet year.

One caution before the beauty: in Spain, totality arrives with the Sun only 3–12 degrees above the west-northwest horizon. A hill, a building, even a stand of trees can erase the whole event. The choreography below assumes you have already found your horizon.

The honest paragraph

What Paris will see — and what it won't

From Paris the Moon takes its first bite at 19:22, and by the 20:17 maximum it covers 92 % of the Sun — a fat crescent of daylight star hanging just 8° above the west-northwest horizon (azimuth 284°). The evening light will turn pewter and strange; shadows will sharpen; the temperature will ease. Gaps between leaves will project a thousand little crescents on the pavement. Through proper eclipse glasses it is a genuinely beautiful sight, made better by its lowness: a horned Sun sinking over the rooftops. The show ends at 21:11 — the very minute of sunset.

But be clear about what 92 % is not. The sky does not go dark. The corona does not appear. No stars come out. Even Madrid's 99.98 % buys none of it — the last 0.02 % of the solar disc is still thousands of times brighter than the full Moon. Totality is binary, and Paris is outside. The band is a two-hour flight or a long day's drive away: Bilbao and Zaragoza both sit inside it. If you can go — go. People who have stood in the umbra do not describe it as an astronomical event; they describe it as a physical one.

19:34 · T − 55 min

First contact

Through glasses: a small black notch on the Sun's north-western limb. To the unaided, unprotected eye nothing whatsoever has changed — which is exactly why the filters matter.

≈ 20:00 · T − 30 min

The light goes wrong

Half the Sun is gone and the world looks like a photograph very slightly underexposed. Colours desaturate. The air begins to cool — over the next half hour the drop can reach several degrees.

≈ 20:14 · T − 15 min

Sharp shadows, little crescents

The Sun is now a narrowing source, so shadows grow strangely crisp. Every gap in the foliage becomes a pinhole camera, projecting crescents. Birds start their evening routines an hour early.

≈ 20:24 · T − 5 min

The plunge

Light drains fast now — perceptibly, minute by minute. Venus snaps out in the southwest. The west-northwest horizon, where the shadow will come from, turns a bruised grey-violet.

≈ 20:27 · T − 2 min

Shadow bands, maybe

On white walls and pale ground, faint rippling bands of light and dark may shimmer — the thin solar crescent twinkling through atmospheric turbulence. Rare, subtle, unphotographable; look sideways.

20:28 · T − 60 s

The umbra arrives

Look northwest: the Moon's shadow is visible as a wall of dusk sweeping toward you across the sky, covering ground faster than sound. This is the moment people gasp. Glasses still ON.

T − 20 s

Baily's beads, then the diamond

The crescent breaks into beads of light between lunar mountains; they wink out one by one until a single point blazes on a ghostly ring — the diamond ring. When it dies —

20:29 · Totality — 1 m 24 s

Filters off. Look up.

A black disc where the Sun was, wreathed in the corona — pearl-white, structured, reaching a solar diameter or more into the sky, in the full-petaled form of a star near activity maximum. For the first seconds a thin ring of crimson chromosphere and perhaps a prominence or two rim the disc. The horizon glows orange for 360°. Venus and Jupiter stand out; Mercury may join them. The temperature has visibly dropped; the world holds its breath. Do not spend it all through a viewfinder.Halfway through, glance behind you: dusk to the southeast, where the shadow is headed.

20:30 · Third contact

The diamond returns — filters ON

A bead of photosphere flares on the opposite limb: totality is over, instantly. Glasses back on before the second flash. The whole sequence now runs in reverse into the low western light.

21:01 – 21:22

Sunset finishes the job

Across eastern Spain the Sun sets before the Moon fully lets go — a partially eclipsed sunset into the Mediterranean. In Galicia the eclipse completes above the horizon at 21:22. Then stay out: the night has one more gift (see below).

Non-negotiable

Your eyes, in five rules

Solar viewing discipline — after the American Astronomical Society & NASA

  1. Filters must be certified ISO 12312-2. Eclipse glasses or handheld solar viewers only — from a vendor on the AAS's audited list. Sunglasses, smoked glass, exposed film, CDs and stacked filters are all useless, and dangerously so.
  2. Inspect before use. Scratched, punctured, torn or delaminating filters go in the bin. Supervise children continuously.
  3. Never point unfiltered optics at the Sun. A camera, binocular or telescope concentrates sunlight enough to destroy a filter behind it — and the retina behind that — in under a second. Optics need their own front-mounted solar filters.
  4. Filters off only inside totality. If — and only if — you stand inside the path, you may (and should) look at the eclipsed Sun with naked eyes between the two diamond rings. The instant photosphere reappears, filters back on. In Paris or Madrid there is no such moment: filters at all times, start to finish.
  5. No filters? Project. A pinhole in a card, or a colander, throws safe crescent images on the ground. You watch the projection, never the hole.

Reference: American Astronomical Society eye-safety guidance (eclipse.aas.org/eye-safety) and NASA eclipse-safety pages.

The encore

Stay out after dark — the Perseids peak the same night

A total eclipse requires a new Moon, and a new Moon means perfectly dark skies. By elegant coincidence, the Perseid meteor shower — the year's most reliable, up to ~100 meteors per hour from a dark site — reaches its peak on the night of 12–13 August 2026, hours after third contact, with zero moonlight to wash it out. An observer in rural Aragón can watch the corona at 20:29 and count fireballs by midnight: two of the sky's great spectacles in one rotation of the Earth.

Face the darkest part of your sky after 23:00; the radiant in Perseus climbs through the night, so the show improves toward dawn. No optics needed — the eclipse rule inverts, and the naked eye is the right instrument. Bring the blanket you sat on for totality.
