IConcept & positioning

Matcha-first, not coffee-with-a-matcha-option.

Paris has learned to queue for matcha. What it has not yet been offered, at scale, is a house — a place built around the leaf the way the great specialty-coffee rooms of the 2010s were built around the bean. Maison Ryokū is that house: a Paris tea company that whisks every bowl to order, bakes its own matcha pâtisserie, sells the same tea it serves, and buys directly from two named farms in Uji and Kirishima. The name is a stylisation of 緑 (ryoku) — green.

Each location is organised as three rooms in one:

  • The Bar. An eight-seat counter where usucha and koicha are whisked to order, and where the latte — the volume driver — is made with the same discipline. The theatre of the house, and the reason people cross the city.
  • The Lab. A small pâtisserie bench at the flagship — matcha financiers, matcha-sesame sablés, nama chocolate — producing for all three houses with a daily morning run. Food lifts the ticket without a kitchen in every site.
  • The Shelf. Retail: 30 g tins of the two farms' matcha, chasen, chawan. Around a tenth of revenue, and the mechanism by which a customer takes the house home — every tin on a Paris kitchen shelf is a standing advertisement.

The positioning is deliberate: craft over speed, supply over hype. In a market where hundreds of coffee shops have added a matcha latte to the board, quality is being commodified faster than it is being understood. Ryokū competes on the two things a menu line cannot copy — a direct, contracted supply of shade-grown tea in the middle of a global shortage, and a room designed for the ritual rather than around an espresso machine.

IIThe market

A global boom, a real shortage, and a city already trained to pay for craft.

Three facts define the opportunity, and all three are measurable. First, the demand curve: third-party estimates put the global matcha market between USD 4.2 and 5.4 billion in 2025 depending on scope, converging on high-single to low-double-digit annual growth into the 2030s — Fortune Business Insights models USD 5.42 billion (2025) growing to USD 10.85 billion by 20346; The Business Research Company puts 2025→2026 growth at 10.6%7; Grand View Research projects a similar trajectory to 20338.

Second, the supply side — the part most plans skip. Japan's green-tea exports in fiscal 2025 (ended March 2026) rose 42% by volume to 13,125 tonnes while their value more than doubled to ¥84.7 billion, with matcha around 70% of volume1. Calendar 2025 was the first year above 10,000 tonnes since 19542. Demand has outrun the tencha fields: auction prices for tencha — the shaded leaf that becomes matcha — rose by as much as 220% in 20254 and roughly doubled again in 2026 to an average of ¥10,843 per kilogram (≈ USD 72)3. As late as February 2026, Kyoto tea shops were still limiting customers to one tin each5. This is the first structural matcha shortage on record — and it is a moat for whoever holds contracted supply, which is precisely what Ryokū's two farm contracts are for (§ IX).

Third, the city. France's premium-coffee wave built the habit Ryokū monetises: roughly 1,200 new coffee shops opened nationally between 2020 and 2024, specialty chains grew from 150 to nearly 500 over 2018–2024, and the premium segment grew 8.7% in 2024 alone10 — a structural shift in urban retail that real-estate advisors now track as its own category11. Parisians already pay EUR 6+ for a crafted drink. And matcha specifically has produced its first French breakout: Milia Matcha, founded in 2023 by creator Andie Ella, reached EUR 3 million of sales in its first year — cited by Forbes as France's number one in Japanese matcha — and its first physical outlet, a month-long pop-up at La Samaritaine in autumn 2025, drew standing queues9. Guides now rank the city's matcha bars the way they ranked its coffee rooms a decade ago15.

The demand read for Ryokū is deliberately bottom-up, not a share of a global TAM: three Paris sites in proven foot-traffic corridors, 145–230 tickets a day each at maturity, an average net ticket of EUR 8.70. That is a EUR 1.74 million run-rate business by month 24 — small against the market numbers above, which is the point: the plan does not require the boom to continue, only for Paris to keep drinking what it already queues for.

The map has an empty middle. On one axis, ceremony — the tea houses hold it, at destination prices and destination pace. On the other, convenience — every coffee bar now pours a green latte of unverifiable grade. The gap is a daily-ritual house at craft quality: faster than a tea room, incomparably better than a menu line, priced between the two. That gap is where specialty coffee built its category in Paris, and it is where Ryokū sits. Our defence against the obvious fast-follower is not the recipe — it is the contracted tencha supply (scarce through at least the 2027 harvests3,4), the trained bar team (§ VII), and three of the best small corners in the city, signed early.

IVUnit economics

One house, four walls, twenty-three points of margin.

Everything in this plan reduces to a repeatable per-location model. A house serves 145–230 tickets a day at maturity depending on format, at an average net ticket of EUR 8.70 (≈ EUR 9.60 incl. VAT, blended across 10% on-site, 5.5% takeaway and 20% retail rates). Cost of goods is modeled at 30% of revenue blended — beverage COGS runs near 20% even at post-shortage tea prices, while retail tins carry ~55% and pastry ~35%. Rents are taken from current Paris asking levels: the Marais averages ~EUR 730/m²/year with prime side-street corners well above13; we model EUR 950–1,030/m²/year all-in for the flagship, and a neighbourhood rent for Batignolles.

Two properties of this model matter more than the margin itself. Each house covers its own four walls at 62% of plan volume — the contribution margin after variable costs is 62 points, so fixed costs of EUR 14.5–23.6k a month break even at 90–143 tickets a day. And the model is priced for the shortage: tea cost assumptions reflect 2026 wholesale tiers after the tencha repricing14, not pre-boom prices. If green tea re-cheapens as new fields mature, that is upside; the plan does not need it.

VThe 24-month build

Three doors in twenty-four months, each cheaper than the last.

The sequence is deliberate: flagship first, in the Haut-Marais, where the brand is made; Saint-Germain second, into the Rive Gauche's tea-room heritage; Batignolles third, a neighbourhood house at a neighbourhood rent, opened once the playbook is proven and partly funded by an equipment loan rather than equity. Fit-out runs three months per site; rent-free periods are negotiated to cover the works.

VICash plan

The low point is December 2027, and it is planned.

Cash opens at EUR 700k in January 2027 — the EUR 600k raise plus EUR 120k of founder capital, net of EUR 20k of formation costs already spent. It never goes below EUR 119.9k, reached in month 12 after two openings have been paid for and before the second house reaches full stride — about 2.2 months of the network's fixed costs at that date. The EUR 180k equipment loan is drawn in April 2028 against the third fit-out, so the third door consumes debt and operating cash flow, not the raise.

VIIPeople

Sixteen and a half people, trained at the whisk.

Two founders carry the company: one runs operations and the tea program (specialty-coffee operations background — sourcing, bar training, multi-site discipline), the other runs brand and finance (luxury-retail background — visual identity, retail economics, landlord negotiations). Both are salaried modestly (EUR 2,800 gross each) until the network is cash-generative; their EUR 120k of capital sits alongside the raise.

Every hire passes through three weeks of tea training — grading, whisking, water temperature, the retail catechism of the two farms — before touching the bar alone. Baristas start at EUR 2,050 gross, roughly 10% above the June-2026 SMIC of EUR 1,867 for a full-time month12, plus pooled tips; store managers at EUR 2,900–3,200. Employer cost is modeled at +31–38% of gross net of the general reduction on low wages.

VIIIMarketing

EUR 105k over 24 months — 5.4% of revenue, front-loaded at each door.

Before each opening. The Milia playbook proved that Paris matcha demand can be manufactured in advance — a month-long department-store pop-up turned an online brand into queues9. Ryokū runs a four-week pop-up counter ahead of the flagship, seeds fifty Paris food and lifestyle creators (matcha is a native short-video category), and opens a waitlist that becomes the CRM. Launch months carry EUR 6k each; the two founders' networks do the rest.

After each opening. The steady budget is EUR 4.5k a month across the network: seasonal drops tied to the harvest calendar (shincha in spring, first-flush tencha in autumn), collaborations with Paris pâtissiers on limited bakes, and the loyalty stamp — the tenth bowl offered. No paid search, no discounting; the product photographs itself and the tins do the rest of the advertising from customers' shelves.

What is deliberately excluded. Wholesale to restaurants and hotels is a real revenue line in this category, and it is not in the plan — neither the revenue nor the working capital. It is held back as upside for year 3, once the third house is open and the supply contracts have a second harvest behind them.

XThe ask

EUR 600k of equity. It builds all three houses — exactly.

The raise is sized to the openings, to the euro: EUR 600k of ordinary equity in the SAS covers the three fit-outs, their equipment, key money, deposits and pre-opening costs. The founders' EUR 120k funds the corporate ramp; a EUR 180k equipment loan, drawn in April 2028 once the network is EBITDA-positive, finances the third house's hardware. Nothing in the plan requires a second raise — house four onward is a choice, fundable from cash flow and debt in 2029.
